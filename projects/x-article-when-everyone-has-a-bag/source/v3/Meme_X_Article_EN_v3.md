# When Everyone Has a Bag, Who Do You Believe?

*Everyone can make a meme famous. Not everyone gets paid when its token
runs.*

When Chill Guy exploded across crypto timelines in November 2024, its
creator, Phillip Banks, had to make an awkward distinction: he had made
the character; he had not authorized a shitcoin built around it.

The token's creator was unknown. Meanwhile, traders were asking Banks to
post a Solana address so they could send him tokens and keep the fun
going. [CoinDesk reported the
dispute.](https://www.coindesk.com/markets/2024/11/21/chillguy-creator-threatens-legal-action-as-crypto-trenches-scour-tiktok)

One image had become three different positions: who creates the culture,
who owns the token, and who collects the upside. The timeline treated
them as one story. They weren't.

**That's the central confusion in memecoins: we call everyone "the
community," even when they contribute, own and earn in very different
ways.**

Memecoins don't just put a price on a joke. They turn the attention
around it into a market signal, then use the resulting price move as new
evidence that the token deserves attention.

## 01 / A meme is open. A token is not.

A meme spreads because people can use it. They copy it, remix it, change
the caption and carry it into a new context. Nobody needs to own the
original image to understand the joke or join the conversation.

A token is different. Its supply is governed by contract rules, its
ownership is uneven, and somebody controls the first allocation. The
moment a ticker attaches to a meme, two audiences start looking at the
same post for different reasons.

One asks: *Is this funny? Is it ours? Can I make something with it?*

The other asks: *What's the CA? Who got in first? How much supply can
hit the market?*

Those audiences can overlap. But cultural reach does not automatically
accrue to a particular token. A meme can be everywhere while its token
has weak distribution, concentrated ownership or a price that already
assumes the next wave of attention.

**A viral meme proves that a culture is spreading. It does not, by
itself, prove that a particular token deserves the bid.**

## 02 / The chart becomes part of the marketing.

The loop is simple. A meme attracts buyers. Buyers move the price. The
green candle becomes a screenshot. The screenshot becomes a post. The
post brings more eyes---and sometimes more buyers.

At that point, the chart is no longer just reporting attention. It is
helping manufacture the next round of it.

A 2026 study by Tomas Jais and Reilly White found that trading activity
in community-driven cryptoassets responds to previous price moves, with
return spillovers across the segment. The research points to a market
where price moves pull in trading activity and shocks spread across the
segment. Price and attention can reinforce one another. [Read the
study.](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0275531926003491)

That changes what an organic timeline actually proves. A post can be
sincere and still be promotional. A holder can genuinely love the meme
and want the next buyer to arrive. The excitement is real; so is the
financial incentive behind it.

**When the same crowd creates the attention and benefits from the price
move, its enthusiasm is not independent confirmation of the trade.**

## 03 / Early to the meme is not early to the supply.

Crypto loves the story of the person who spots a joke before everyone
else. Sometimes that person really is early. But discovering a meme
early and entering a token early are two different advantages.

Bitquery examined 8,212,568 Pump.fun launches with first trades between
October 30, 2025 and October 7, 2026. Under its wallet-classification
method, developer-linked wallets bought in the launch block on 21% of
launches. The developer's first sale occurred within one minute on 65%
of launches. Read those figures as evidence of early positioning, not as
a fraud tally. The ownership story can begin before a token reaches the
wider timeline. [Bitquery's
investigation.](https://www.bitquery.io/investigations/pump-fun-snipers-bundles)

You can discover a token ten minutes after launch and still be entering
a market where the earliest positions were built before you arrived.
Then X fills with posts saying the community has found it. Maybe it has.
But community attention does not tell you who is sitting on the earliest
supply.

A market-cap figure takes a marginal trade price and multiplies it by a
reported supply figure. It is not the cash that entered, and it does not
mean every holder can exit at that price.

**The crowd may arrive together on the timeline. It did not necessarily
arrive together in the cap table.**

## 04 / The community can be real---and still be selling.

The debate usually collapses into two lazy camps.

The cynic sees a financial incentive and decides the culture must be
fake. The believer sees real people making memes and decides the market
must be fair.

Neither conclusion follows.

One holder can spend nights making edits, translating posts, moderating
a chat and helping strangers. The same person can hope the token rises
because they own it. That's not a contradiction. It's a person with more
than one motive.

The mistake is treating visible conviction as neutral evidence. If the
people posting also benefit when you buy, their enthusiasm may be
heartfelt---but it is still part of the market's sales process.

**The crowd can be real and still be a biased witness. A hundred posts
from holders are not automatically a hundred independent votes for the
token's value.**

## 05 / Three ledgers are hiding inside one word: "community."

To understand who benefits, separate three things.

1.  **Culture:** Who made the original meme? Who creates the edits,
    translations, events and inside jokes that keep it alive?

2.  **Ownership:** Who received the supply, when did they get it, how
    concentrated is it, and who can sell into new attention?

3.  **Cash flow:** Who receives creator fees, holder rewards or other
    payments tied to trading activity?

These ledgers do not automatically line up. Pump.fun's current
documentation distinguishes creator fees from Holder Rewards: a token
can route the creator fee to its creator, or direct that fee to eligible
holders instead. The fee recipient is a token-level choice; it is not
automatically the person who made the original meme or did the most
community work. [Pump.fun fee
documentation.](https://pump.fun/docs/fees)

Early capital can deserve a return. Creative labor can create value. Fee
income can reward the token creator or eligible holders. Those are
different contributions and should be described separately.

**The problem is pretending they are the same reward.**

My view is straightforward: a fairer memecoin market does not need to
make every participant earn the same amount. It needs to make the claims
visible. Who created the culture? Who owns the token? Who gets paid when
people trade? If those answers are hidden behind the word "community,"
the word is doing the work of a marketing department.

## 06 / Dogecoin showed what a meme community can do.

In 2014, a Dogecoin community fundraiser raised more than \$30,000 to
help Jamaica's bobsled team travel to the Sochi Winter Olympics. [PBS
covered the
campaign.](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/world/jamaican-bobsled-team-raises-30000-in-dogecoin-for-trip-to-sochi)

That was more than a chart. People took a shared internet identity and
used it to coordinate a real-world outcome. Money did not destroy the
culture; it gave the culture a way to act.

The fundraiser shows what a meme community can do when shared identity
turns into coordinated action. It does not prove that a token attached
to another meme is fairly priced. Culture can create real outcomes;
token ownership still has to be judged on its own terms.

**The goal should not be to remove money from meme culture. It should be
to stop money from disguising who creates the value and who captures
it.**

## 07 / What I would look for in the next lasting memecoin.

I would look for two engines working together:

1.  **A cultural engine:** people keep remixing the meme, making things,
    welcoming newcomers and organizing around it when the chart is
    boring. The token is not the only reason to participate.

2.  **An economic engine:** supply, early allocations and fee recipients
    are legible. The people building the culture are not simply told
    that "community" is their reward while someone else owns the claims
    and collects the cash flow.

A meme with only the cultural engine can become a lasting internet joke
without becoming a valuable token. A token with only the financial
engine is a ticker wearing a meme as packaging. **The rare winner is the
one that turns cultural participation into durable coordination without
confusing attention with ownership.**

So don't ask only whether a memecoin community is real. Ask whether it
still creates something when the chart goes flat---and whether you can
see who benefits when the chart goes up.

The next big meme should give people more than a reason to buy. It
should give them a reason to stay, and a clear account of how the value
they help create is shared.

**That's the standard I would use.**

Which ledger do you check first: who creates the culture, who holds the
supply, or who receives the fees?
