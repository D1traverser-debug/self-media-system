# When Everyone Has a Bag, Who Do You Believe?

*Everyone can make a meme famous. Not everyone gets paid when its token runs.*

When Chill Guy exploded across crypto timelines in November 2024, its creator, Phillip Banks, drew a line between the character he had made and commercial uses he had not authorized.

CoinDesk reported that Banks said he would issue takedown notices for unauthorized merchandise and shitcoins, while saying he was less concerned about brands using the character as a trend. The creator of the CHILLGUY token was unknown. At the same time, crypto traders were jokingly asking Banks to post a Solana address so they could send him money or tokens and keep the fun going. [CoinDesk reported the dispute.](https://www.coindesk.com/markets/2024/11/21/chillguy-creator-threatens-legal-action-as-crypto-trenches-scour-tiktok)

One image had become the basis for three very different claims: who creates the culture, who owns the token, and who captures the financial upside. The timeline treated them as one story. They weren't.

**That's the central confusion in memecoins: we call everyone "the community," even when they contribute, own and earn in very different ways.**

Memecoins don't just put a price on a joke. They turn the attention around it into a market signal, then use the resulting price move as new evidence that the token deserves attention.

## 01 / A meme travels. A token has a cap table.

A meme spreads because people can use it. They copy it, remix it, change the caption and carry it into a new context. You don't need to own the original image to join the conversation—but the creator's authorship does not disappear as the meme travels.

A token is different. Its supply is governed by contract rules, its ownership is uneven, and somebody controls the first allocation. The moment a ticker attaches to a meme, two audiences start looking at the same post for different reasons.

One asks: *Is this funny? Is it ours? Can I make something with it?*

The other asks: *What's the CA? Who got in first? How much supply can hit the market?*

Those audiences can overlap. But cultural reach does not automatically accrue to a particular token. A meme can leave a Bigfoot-sized footprint across the timeline while its token still has weak distribution, concentrated ownership or a price that already assumes the next wave of attention.

**A viral meme proves that a culture is spreading. It does not, by itself, prove that a particular token deserves the bid.**

## 02 / The chart becomes part of the marketing.

The loop is simple. A meme attracts buyers. Buyers move the price. The green candle becomes a screenshot. The screenshot becomes a post. The post brings more eyes—and sometimes more buyers.

At that point, the chart is no longer just reporting attention. It is helping produce the next round of it.

A 2026 study by Tomas Jais and Reilly White, using daily data from 2021 through March 2026, found that memecoin turnover was sensitive to salient price movements and that returns spilled over within the segment. These are market-level patterns consistent with attention-linked demand—not proof that a particular post caused a particular rally. [Read the study.](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0275531926003491)

That complicates what an organic timeline actually proves. A post can be sincere and still be promotional. A holder can genuinely love the meme and hope the next buyer arrives. The excitement can be real; so can the financial incentive behind it.

**When the same crowd creates attention and may benefit from the resulting price move, its enthusiasm is not independent confirmation of the trade.**

## 03 / Early to the meme is not early to the supply.

Crypto loves the story of the person who spots a joke before everyone else. Sometimes that person really is early. But discovering a meme early and entering a token early are two different advantages.

Bitquery examined 8,212,568 Pump.fun launches whose first trades fell between October 30, 2025 and October 7, 2026. Under its on-chain linkage methodology, developer-linked wallets appeared among buyers in the launch block on 21% of launches. Across the full sample, the developer's first sale occurred within one minute of launch on 65% of launches. [Bitquery's investigation.](https://www.bitquery.io/investigations/pump-fun-snipers-bundles)

Bitquery identifies developer-linked wallets through transaction timing and funding signals; that is not verified real-world identity data. And a first sale is not evidence of a complete exit. Read these figures as evidence of early positioning and rapid first-sale activity—not as a scam rate.

You can discover a token ten minutes after launch and still be entering a market where the earliest positions were built before you arrived. Then X fills with posts saying the community has found it. Maybe it has. But community attention does not tell you who is sitting on the earliest supply.

A market-cap figure typically multiplies a marginal market price by a reported supply figure. It is not the cash that entered the market, and it does not mean every holder can exit at that price.

**The crowd may arrive together on the timeline. It did not necessarily arrive together in the cap table.**

## 04 / The community can be real—and still be selling.

The debate usually collapses into two camps.

The cynic sees a financial incentive and decides the culture must be fake. The believer sees real people making memes and decides the market must be fair.

Neither conclusion follows.

One holder can spend nights making edits, translating posts, moderating a chat and helping strangers. The same person can hope the token rises because they own it. That's not a contradiction. It's a person with more than one motive.

The mistake is treating visible conviction as neutral evidence. If the people posting hold the token, or otherwise benefit when others buy, their enthusiasm may be heartfelt. But they are interested participants, not independent observers.

**The crowd can be real and still be a biased witness. A hundred posts from holders are not automatically a hundred independent votes for the token's value.**

## 05 / Three ledgers are hiding inside one word: "community."

To understand who benefits, separate three things.

1. **Culture:** Who made the original meme? Who creates the edits, translations, events and inside jokes that keep it alive?

2. **Ownership:** Who received the supply, when did they get it, how concentrated is it, and who can sell into new attention?

3. **Cash flow:** Who receives creator fees, holder rewards or other payments tied to trading activity?

These ledgers do not automatically line up. Pump.fun's documentation says a creator can choose at launch to direct the Creator fee to eligible holders instead of to the creator. Holder Rewards are subject to eligibility thresholds, platform rules and distribution costs; they are not a guaranteed royalty. The fee recipient is a token-level configuration, not automatically the person who made the original meme or did the most community work. [Pump.fun fee documentation.](https://pump.fun/docs/fees)

Early capital can deserve a return. Creative labor can create value. Fee income can reward the creator or eligible holders, depending on the token's configuration. Those are different contributions and claims, and they should be described separately.

**The problem is pretending they are the same reward.**

My view is straightforward: a fairer memecoin market does not need to make every participant earn the same amount. It needs to make the claims visible. Who created the culture? Who owns the token? Who gets paid when people trade? If those answers are hidden behind the word "community," the word is doing the work of a marketing department.

## 06 / Dogecoin showed what a meme community can do.

In 2014, a Dogecoin fundraiser known as Dogesled raised more than $30,000 for Jamaica's two-man bobsled team to travel to the Sochi Winter Olympics. [PBS covered the fundraiser.](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/world/jamaican-bobsled-team-raises-30000-in-dogecoin-for-trip-to-sochi)

That was more than a chart. People took a shared internet identity and used it to coordinate a real-world outcome. Money did not destroy the culture; it gave the culture a way to act.

The fundraiser shows that a meme community can turn shared identity into coordinated action. It does not prove that a token attached to another meme is fairly priced, or that its value is fairly distributed. Culture can create real outcomes; token ownership still has to be judged on its own terms.

**The goal should not be to remove money from meme culture. It should be to stop money from disguising who creates the value and who captures it.**

## 07 / What I would look for in the next lasting memecoin.

I would look for two engines working together:

1. **A cultural engine:** people keep remixing the meme, making things, welcoming newcomers and organizing around it when the chart is boring. The token is not the only reason to participate.

2. **An economic engine:** supply, early allocations, wallet concentration and fee recipients are legible. The people building the culture can see what they own, how their work is rewarded and who receives the cash flow. "Community" should not be used as a substitute for explaining who holds the claims.

A meme with only the cultural engine can become a lasting internet joke without becoming a valuable token. A token with only the financial engine is a ticker wearing a meme as packaging. The stronger project is one that turns cultural participation into durable coordination without confusing attention with ownership.

A community is not a single stakeholder. It is a meeting point for people who create, hold, promote and get paid—sometimes the same people, often not.

The next big meme should give people more than a reason to buy. It should give them a reason to stay, and a clear account of how the value they help create is shared.

**That's the difference between building a market around a meme and building something people actually want to belong to.**

Which ledger do you check first: culture, ownership, or cash flow?
