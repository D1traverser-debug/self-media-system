# When Everyone Has a Bag, Who Do You Believe?

*Everyone can make a meme famous. Not everyone gets paid when its token runs.*

On November 21, 2024, Phillip Banks got an unusual request: post a Solana address so traders could send him money or tokens.

A Solana memecoin using the Chill Guy character he had created was surging. Banks said the character was copyrighted and that he planned to issue takedowns for unauthorized merchandise and shitcoins. Brand accounts using it as a trend were different; he mainly wanted credit. The token's creator was unknown. [CoinDesk reported the exchange.](https://www.coindesk.com/markets/2024/11/21/chillguy-creator-threatens-legal-action-as-crypto-trenches-scour-tiktok)

One person made the image. Someone else launched the coin. Traders wanted the artist to accept a cut. They were all talking about the same meme, but they did not have the same claim on it.

That distinction disappears when everyone gets folded into one word: **community**. But a buyer still needs to know whose interests that word is representing.

## 01 / A meme can travel. Its token is a separate bet.

A meme spreads because people can use it. They copy it, remix it, change the caption and carry it into a new context. You don't need to own the original image to join the joke. But the freedom to remix a meme is not the same as owning its character—or the token someone builds around it.

A ticker adds a second game to the same image. One person sees a joke worth remixing; another sees a CA, a launch price and a supply distribution. Sometimes it's the same person, just looking at the meme from two different angles.

An image can leave a Bigfoot-sized footprint across X and still be attached to a coin with concentrated supply or a price that already assumes another wave of buyers.

**Virality tells you people noticed the meme. It doesn't tell you who owns the token—or whether the token captures any of that attention.**

## 02 / The chart starts advertising the meme.

Watch what happens after a post catches. Buyers lift the price. Someone screenshots the green candle. The screenshot goes back into the feed as proof that the meme is moving. More eyes arrive; some become buyers.

Soon the chart is doing two jobs: recording attention and selling the next wave.

A November 2026 study by Tomas Jais and Reilly White finds stronger spillovers and attention-driven trading among community tokens. That points to a feedback-prone market. It does not prove that every rally is manufactured or that a particular post caused one. [Read the study.](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0275531926003491)

**A post can be sincere and promotional at the same time.** If I own the coin, a new buyer is both a reader and a source of demand. Neither fact cancels the other.

I wouldn't call every excited holder a shill. But I wouldn't use their enthusiasm as independent evidence, either.

## 03 / You can be early to the post and late to the launch.

Bitquery examined 8,212,568 Pump.fun launches whose first trades occurred between October 30, 2025 and October 7, 2026. Using its on-chain rules for identifying developer wallets, it found those wallets buying in the launch block on 21% of launches. On 65% of launches, the developer's first sale came within one minute. [Read Bitquery's investigation.](https://www.bitquery.io/investigations/pump-fun-snipers-bundles)

Those are wallet classifications, not verified identities. Bitquery links wallets through the launch transaction, fee payment and the four transactions immediately after launch; a first sale also does not mean the whole position was sold. The figures are not a scam rate.

The timing still matters. By the time a token reaches your X feed, its first ownership positions may already be in place. **You may be early to the meme and late to the trade.**

Market cap is a quote, not a cash balance. It multiplies a market price by a supply figure; it doesn't tell you how much money entered, or how much can leave at that price.

## 04 / Real people can give you a biased signal.

Some holders do the work that makes a community feel alive: fan art, translations, moderation, welcome messages, spaces, raids and the daily replies that keep a meme in circulation. Some genuinely care about the project. Some also want the price to rise. Often, both are true.

Calling that fake misses the point. Treating it as neutral evidence misses it too.

Read the incentive alongside the post. Does the person disclose a position? Are they showing work beyond the chart? Do they stick around when volume fades? You can't read someone's mind from a post, but you can check what they disclose and whether they keep contributing when trading slows.

## 05 / "Community" is three different ledgers.

Before trusting the word, separate three questions:

1. **Culture:** Who made the original meme? Who keeps it alive through edits, translations, events and inside jokes?

2. **Ownership:** Who received the supply, when did they get it, how concentrated is it, and who can sell into new attention?

3. **Cash flow:** Who receives creator fees, holder rewards or other payments when people trade?

Pump.fun is one example, not a universal template. Its current documentation says a creator may choose at launch to direct the Creator fee to eligible holders instead of the creator. Holder Rewards have eligibility rules and distribution costs; payments are not guaranteed. The person who made the meme and the wallet receiving trading fees can be two different parties. [Pump.fun's fee documentation.](https://pump.fun/docs/fees)

The early buyer supplied capital; the artist supplied the character; the moderator supplied labor; a different wallet may collect the fees. Those contributions do not have to pay equally. They do need to be named accurately.

**A buyer should be able to tell which role is being rewarded before being asked to believe in the whole community.**

## 06 / Dogecoin is why I won't write off community.

In 2014, Dogecoin supporters organized the Dogesled fundraiser and raised more than $30,000 in Dogecoin for Jamaica's two-man bobsled team to travel to the Sochi Winter Olympics. [PBS covered the fundraiser.](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/world/jamaican-bobsled-team-raises-30000-in-dogecoin-for-trip-to-sochi)

That money did something outside the chart. People took a shared internet identity and helped a real team get to the Games.

It doesn't prove that another meme token is fairly priced, or that its holders share value fairly. It does make the blanket claim that money automatically makes a community fake much harder to defend.

## 07 / What would make me trust a memecoin?

I wouldn't start by counting followers or posts. I'd ask three things.

Does the culture keep producing when the chart goes sideways? Can I inspect who owns the supply and when they got it? Can I see where the trading fees go—and whether the people promoting the coin disclose their positions?

A lasting memecoin needs people who would still make things when the chart stops giving them a reason. It also needs an ownership and fee structure that doesn't hide who benefits when the price moves.

That's a high bar. But the word "community" shouldn't lower it.

**A meme can belong to the internet. A token belongs to its holders.** If the pitch blurs those two facts, don't let the crowd make the decision for you.

Which do you check first: culture, supply or cash flow?
