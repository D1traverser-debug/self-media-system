# When Everyone Has a Bag, Who Do You Believe?

*Everyone can make a meme famous. Not everyone gets paid when its token runs.*

In November 2024, a Crypto Twitter trader offered a simple solution to the man who drew Chill Guy: ask for 2% of the token supply. “As is tradition,” he joked.

The joke landed because everyone understood the arrangement it was suggesting. Phillip Banks had created the character. Someone else had launched the Solana token. Traders wanted the artist to accept a piece of a market he hadn't built, and perhaps stop objecting to it.

Banks had other ideas. He said the character was copyrighted and warned of takedowns for unauthorized merchandise and shitcoins. Brand accounts joining the trend were a different matter; he mainly wanted credit. Meanwhile, the token had reportedly touched a **$500 million market cap** and then fallen nearly halfway from its peak. [CoinDesk documented the exchange.](https://www.coindesk.com/markets/2024/11/21/chillguy-creator-threatens-legal-action-as-crypto-trenches-scour-tiktok)

An artist was talking about his work. Traders were talking about a percentage. They were looking at the same image and seeing very different things.

That is where the word **community** gets slippery. It can describe the people who keep a joke alive, the wallets holding a coin, or the people who collect money when that coin trades. Sometimes they're the same people. Often, the overlap is the part nobody has bothered to check.

## The joke travels. The ownership stays on-chain.

A meme has no single route through the internet. Someone redraws it. Somebody turns it into a reply. A bigger account reposts it, and an audience that has never opened a crypto wallet joins the joke. The image can keep moving long after the original post disappears from view.

A token moves differently. It has a contract address, a supply and a distribution of wallets. It can carry the same image without distributing ownership to the people who made that image popular.

A screenshot of a Bigfoot-sized green candle can travel farther than the joke that gave a coin its name. Some viewers meet the price before they ever see the original post. By then, the chart has become part of the meme's identity.

That matters because attention has become something holders can trade on. A screenshot of a rising price draws an audience; some of that audience buys; the new trades give holders another screenshot to circulate. The loop can sustain itself for a while, with a hundred sincere participants and no single person directing it.

A 2026 study by Tomas Jais and Reilly White finds that community-driven tokens show attention-linked trading, return spillovers within the segment and elevated illiquidity and downside risk. Its evidence concerns patterns across tokens, where attention and price can feed into each other. [Read the research.](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0275531926003491)

The feed can fill with excitement much faster than a market can absorb sell orders. A huge audience and an easy exit are two very different assets.

## Your first sighting may be someone else's exit.

In a study of **8,212,568 Pump.fun launches** with first trades from October 30, 2025, to October 7, 2026, Bitquery found that the developer's first sale occurred within one minute on **65%** of launches. It also found developer-linked wallets buying in the launch block on **21%** of launches. [The investigation explains its methodology.](https://www.bitquery.io/investigations/pump-fun-snipers-bundles)

Those labels come from on-chain transaction patterns, not verified real-world identities. A first sale also doesn't establish that the developer sold everything. What the data captures is how early trading and the public discovery of a token can operate on entirely different clocks.

Picture the two timelines. On one, wallets are buying and selling in the opening seconds. On the other, a funny clip is accumulating reposts, somebody notices the ticker, and the token finally reaches a new reader's feed. The people in those timelines can be trading the same coin with completely different entry prices and reasons to act.

The displayed market cap doesn't reconcile those differences. It multiplies a quoted price by a supply figure. The value a large holder can actually realize depends on the orders waiting on the other side of a sale.

A holder who posts enthusiastically may believe every word. They may also benefit if that post brings the next buyer. A reader can take the person's contribution seriously while reading the position behind the post as part of the story.

## The people building the culture can be real. So can their incentives.

The most convincing communities usually have something happening between price updates: new artwork, better jokes, translation, moderation, spaces, meetups, charity projects, the small acts that keep people returning. A token can give some contributors a financial reason to do more of that work. That doesn't make the work imaginary.

But the payment system is a separate piece of the story.

On Pump.fun, for example, a token creator can choose at launch to send the Creator fee to eligible token holders through a Holder Rewards arrangement instead of receiving it personally. Eligibility rules and distribution costs apply, and payments aren't guaranteed. [The platform documents the fee settings.](https://pump.fun/docs/fees)

The word *creator* in a fee setting refers to a platform role. It may have little to do with who made the original character, joke or artwork. The person drawing the meme, the person deploying the token and the wallet receiving fees can be three different parties.

Crypto has also produced evidence that a meme community can coordinate around something tangible. In 2014, Dogecoin supporters raised more than **$30,000** to help Jamaica's two-man bobsled team reach the Sochi Winter Olympics. The Dogesled fundraiser gave people a reason to act together that extended well beyond a trading chart. [PBS reported the campaign.](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/world/jamaican-bobsled-team-raises-30000-in-dogecoin-for-trip-to-sochi)

The fundraiser turned an online identity into a real contribution. Cultural value can be created and shared widely even when the financial gains from a related token sit in a narrower set of wallets.

The distinction changes how I read a memecoin pitch. I look for the work people keep doing when the price stalls. I look at when major wallets received their supply. I look at whether the accounts amplifying the story disclose a position, and where the trading fees actually go. Those details make the enthusiasm easier to interpret.

Banks was defending his character. The trader answered with a percentage of token supply. Two years later, that exchange still captures how easily the culture around a meme gets mistaken for the economics of its coin.

**The meme can belong to the conversation. The trade belongs to whoever holds the position.** The closer someone moves from celebrating the first to selling you the second, the more that difference matters.
