# When Everyone Has a Bag, Who Do You Believe?

*Everyone can make a meme famous. Not everyone gets paid when its token runs.*

During the 2024 Chill Guy frenzy, one Crypto Twitter trader had a suggestion for the character's creator: ask for 2% of the token supply. “As is tradition,” he joked.

The joke was more revealing than the percentage. Phillip Banks had created the character; an unknown issuer had launched the Solana token; traders wanted Banks to step into the story. His attention could help the token travel, and the market already understood that attention had economic value.

Banks said the character was copyrighted and that he planned takedowns for unauthorized merchandise and shitcoins. Brand accounts using the character as a trend were different; he mainly wanted credit. [CoinDesk reported the exchange.](https://www.coindesk.com/markets/2024/11/21/chillguy-creator-threatens-legal-action-as-crypto-trenches-scour-tiktok)

The same meme had become several different things: a creative work, a social identity, a tradable asset and a possible source of fees. The people creating its culture, holding its supply and receiving its payments could be different people. The word **community** tends to blur those distinctions.

## The meme travels farther than the token.

A meme can be copied, remixed and carried into new audiences. A Bigfoot joke can circulate across X without anyone needing permission from a token holder. The token built around a meme has a finite supply, a launch history and a holder distribution.

That difference becomes important when price turns into content. A holder posts a rising chart; the screenshot reaches a new audience; some viewers buy; their orders create another move worth posting. The chart starts feeding the attention that helped move it.

Recent research on community-driven tokens finds that trading activity responds to salient price movements, returns spill across tokens in the same segment, and these assets exhibit higher illiquidity and downside tail risk. [The study examines those market dynamics.](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0275531926003491)

A holder can genuinely like the meme, contribute to the community and own the token at the same time. Their enthusiasm can be sincere while their post also helps attract the next buyer. Those motives coexist; the post is still coming from someone with a position.

## A position changes the meaning of a post.

An on-chain investigation of 8,212,568 Pump.fun launches found that the developer's first sale occurred within one minute on 65% of launches. The wallet labels are inferred from launch transactions, fee payments and subsequent activity, not verified real-world identities; a first sale does not establish a full exit. [Bitquery's investigation explains its method.](https://www.bitquery.io/investigations/pump-fun-snipers-bundles)

The timing matters. By the time a token reaches the public feed, its earliest ownership positions may already be in place. A viral post tells you that attention has arrived. It does not tell you who was positioned before the post, how much supply they hold or how easily they can sell.

Market cap is a quoted price multiplied by supply. It is a valuation snapshot, not the amount of money that entered the token or the liquidity available to exit at that price.

Wanting a higher price is ordinary self-interest. Opportunism is different: it appears when someone hides a position, presents a conflicted pitch as independent judgment, or withholds information that would change how a buyer reads the promotion. A disclosed holding is a conflict to account for, not proof of dishonesty. A manufactured appearance of neutrality is a different signal.

## Follow the work, the supply and the fees.

The cultural record lives in the work people keep doing: original posts, art, translations, spaces, moderation, events and jokes that survive a quiet chart. The ownership record lives in the distribution: who received the tokens, when they received them and how concentrated the supply remains. The cash-flow record lives in the fee settings and the wallets that receive payments.

Those records can overlap. An artist can hold tokens; an early buyer can become a real organizer; a moderator can receive rewards. The point is not to rank those people. It is to see which contribution is being made, which claim is being sold and who receives the economic benefit.

Pump.fun provides one example of how the cash-flow side can work. Its documentation allows a creator to direct the Creator fee to eligible holders instead of receiving it personally. Holder Rewards have eligibility requirements and distribution costs, and payments are not guaranteed. The platform's setting does not automatically create a royalty for the person who made the original meme. [Pump.fun's fee documentation.](https://pump.fun/docs/fees)

Online communities can also produce value beyond trading. Dogecoin supporters raised more than $30,000 for Jamaica's two-man bobsled team to travel to the 2014 Sochi Winter Olympics. That fundraiser showed real coordination; it did not determine how a separate token's supply or fees were distributed. [PBS covered the fundraiser.](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/world/jamaican-bobsled-team-raises-30000-in-dogecoin-for-trip-to-sochi)

A busy timeline is evidence of activity. It is not proof of broad ownership, independent demand or shared upside. Read the work to understand the culture, the holder distribution to understand ownership, and the fee route to understand who gets paid.

The meme's reach is public. The token's upside is allocated. **The word community cannot tell you that split; the records can.**
