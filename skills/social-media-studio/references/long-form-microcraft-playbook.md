## 1. The microcraft model

Microcraft is not synonymous with short sentences or decorative prose. It is the control of reader processing at four connected scales:

- **Sentence:** what is the sentence about, what action or relation occurs, what is new, and what should land with emphasis?
- **Paragraph:** what single dominant move does this unit make, and how does each sentence advance it?
- **Transition:** what exact logical or emotional relation connects this unit to the next?
- **Detail / rhetorical device:** what concrete evidence, image, contrast, pattern or sound makes the idea easier to see, remember, question or feel?

These units interact. A sentence can be elegant in isolation but fail because its opening does not connect to the previous sentence. A paragraph can be cohesive sentence by sentence yet wander away from its point. A vivid metaphor can increase memorability while introducing a false equivalence. A short line can create emphasis once and become mannerism when repeated every paragraph.

The governing question is not “Does this sound impressive?” It is: **What does this choice make the reader understand, notice, infer, feel or do—and what cost does it impose?**

## 2. Openings: earn attention and establish a contract

### 2.1 Opening is a sequence, not a magic first line

The title, subtitle, first screen, early orientation and first meaningful payoff form one acquisition-and-retention system. A striking first sentence cannot compensate for a body that does not deliver its promise.

A strong opening may perform some of these jobs, in an order suited to the material:

1. **Anchor:** give the reader something specific to see or understand: an event, number, decision, contradiction, person, mechanism, result or question.
2. **Disruption:** expose what is unusual, unresolved, consequential or counterintuitive.
3. **Stake:** show why this matters to the intended reader or to the people involved.
4. **Orientation:** identify enough context, actors and scope to prevent confusion.
5. **Promise:** reveal the article's real subject or central question, not just its topic.
6. **Early proof:** give enough substance to demonstrate that the article has more than an attractive headline.
7. **Remaining payoff:** preserve a material question whose answer is worth continuing for.

Not every opening needs all seven explicitly. The required subset depends on genre, reader familiarity, evidence and platform.

### 2.2 Opening families — choose by material

| Opening family | Useful when | Typical engine | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reported moment / scene | A verified action or decision contains the larger issue | Moment → anomaly / choice → wider meaning | Invented interiority, irrelevant cinematic detail |
| Hard contradiction | Two facts, outcomes or expectations conflict | A says X; observed evidence shows Y | Artificial paradox or misleading number |
| Concrete consequence | Reader needs to feel the real cost of an abstract system | Specific outcome → mechanism behind it | Emotional manipulation or one case overgeneralized |
| Mechanism reveal | The article's value is explaining how something works | Familiar result → hidden mechanism → consequence | Starting too abstractly or explaining too much too soon |
| Question / dilemma | The reader faces a real decision or uncertainty | Choice → competing costs → decision criteria | Generic rhetorical question with no answer debt |
| Direct thesis | The reader wants a clear argument, not a narrative entrance | Bounded claim → reason it matters → proof path | Overclaiming before evidence appears |
| Comparative split | Two similar cases diverge | Same condition → different outcomes → explanatory variable | A comparison without controlled criteria |

A “hook” is not necessarily a question or cliffhanger. It is a credible reason to enter and continue.

### 2.3 The nut / orientation contract

For narrative and feature writing, a nut graf or equivalent orientation tells the reader what the story is really about, why it matters and what larger frame the opening moment belongs to. It need not be a single paragraph immediately after the first sentence. It must arrive early enough that curiosity does not become disorientation.

For argument and explainer writing, the equivalent is a clear thesis / reader contract: what question is being answered, what the writer contributes and what the reader should be able to understand by the end.

Diagnostic questions:

- If the title is hidden, can a cold reader identify the article's real subject and value from the opening portion?
- Does the opening provide a concrete reason to care, or merely announce importance?
- Is the promised question actually answered later?
- Does the article provide early evidence of substance?
- Is anything withheld that the reader needs simply to understand the premise?
- Could a different opening be more truthful, direct or valuable for this genre?

### 2.4 Opening ablation

Test materially different openings, not synonym variants. Compare a scene-led, contradiction-led, mechanism-led or thesis-led version when the material permits. Remove the opening's most dramatic sentence: if the reader contract collapses, the piece may be depending on bait rather than substance. Conversely, remove all scene-setting: if nothing meaningful is lost, the scene may be decorative.

Do not impose “first three sentences” or a universal paragraph count. The first-screen area is a cold-reader diagnostic zone, not a syntax template.

## 3. Sentence engineering

### 3.1 Topic, character and action

Readers need to know what a sentence is about before they can process what happens to it. When the material has an actor, make the actor visible in the grammatical subject when that improves clarity. Put the action in a strong verb when the action itself matters.

Diagnostic pattern:

- Who or what is the sentence about?
- What is it doing, experiencing, changing or being affected by?
- Does the grammatical subject match the conceptual topic?
- Does the verb carry the main action, or has the action been buried in an abstract noun?

Example:

- Dense: `The implementation of the pricing change resulted in a reduction of customer churn.`
- More direct: `The new pricing reduced customer churn.`
- If the process itself is the topic: `The implementation took six months; churn did not fall until the final pricing tier changed.`

The rule is not “delete all nominalizations.” Terms such as *distribution, governance, liquidity, memory* or *ownership* may be necessary conceptual objects. Retain them when the concept is the subject of analysis; convert them to verbs when they hide an actor or action that the reader needs to see.

### 3.2 Given → new information flow

A useful default is to begin with information the reader already knows, or that the previous sentence has just established, and move toward the new information. This creates a handhold and lets the new idea arrive at the sentence's emphasis point.

Diagnostic:

1. Underline the first meaningful topic of each sentence.
2. Ask whether it links to the prior sentence or to the reader's known context.
3. Underline the sentence's most important new information.
4. Ask whether the new information arrives where the sentence can emphasize it.

This is a default, not an absolute. A deliberate surprise, scene entrance, contrast or urgent new fact may justify beginning with new information. The test is whether the reader can connect it without backtracking.

### 3.3 Stress position and sentence landing

The end of a sentence often receives emphasis. Put the information that deserves emphasis there, rather than leaving an incidental clause or a weak administrative phrase as the landing point.

Compare:

- `After the team changed the routing policy, latency fell by 30%.`
- `Latency fell by 30% after the team changed the routing policy.`

Neither is universally better. The first foregrounds the action and ends on the result; the second begins with the result and ends on the proposed cause. Choose according to the paragraph's current topic and the claim's evidential status. If the causal link is not established, do not let syntax imply certainty that the evidence cannot support.

A sentence ending can also carry a contrast, qualification, image or implication. Do not make every sentence land on a punchline; emphasis loses force when everything is emphasized.

### 3.4 Clause hierarchy and processing load

A sentence becomes difficult when it asks the reader to hold too many unfamiliar concepts, nested qualifications or competing actions before revealing the main relationship.

Revision sequence:

1. Find the main clause and its verb.
2. Identify which clause supplies the essential condition, cause, contrast or qualification.
3. Move background that interrupts the main action to a more useful position.
4. Split when two or more new conceptual steps need separate processing.
5. Preserve a longer sentence when its syntax expresses a real relationship that would be weakened by chopping it apart.

Do not equate long sentences with bad writing. The issue is the number and arrangement of new relations the reader must track.

### 3.5 Active and passive voice

Prefer active voice when it makes a consequential actor and action visible. Use passive voice when the receiver of the action is the paragraph's established topic, when the actor is genuinely unknown or immaterial, or when passive order preserves a clearer given-to-new flow.

Never delete an actor merely to sound neutral. Never force an actor into the sentence if the evidence does not identify one. Passive voice is a focus and information-order choice, not a moral failure.

### 3.6 Precision, abstraction and modifiers

- Prefer a precise noun or verb over a stack of vague modifiers.
- Use adjectives and adverbs when they distinguish a meaningful property, not to intensify a sentence that lacks evidence.
- Replace vague abstractions with observable behavior when the behavior is known and relevant.
- Keep an abstraction when it names the mechanism more accurately than a list of examples.
- Avoid throat-clearing that announces the next thought without adding it.

Review trigger phrases include “it is important to note,” “it should be emphasized,” “there is a need for,” “it can be seen that,” and repeated “not X but Y” scaffolding. They are not banned words. Remove or rewrite only when they substitute for a claim, actor, reason or piece of evidence.

### 3.7 Sentence rhythm

Rhythm should follow thought and reader processing:

- longer syntax can carry qualification, comparison, accumulation or a complex causal relation;
- shorter syntax can isolate a result, interruption, decision or consequence;
- parallel syntax can make comparable items easier to perceive;
- a sentence fragment can create a deliberate beat when the context supports it;
- a full sentence may be better when a fragment would obscure agency or logic.

Do not randomize sentence length, impose short-sentence quotas or break every sentence into a standalone paragraph to simulate internet style. Vary rhetorical function first; sentence length follows.

## 4. Paragraph engineering

### 4.1 A paragraph is a unit of thought, not a fixed number of sentences

A paragraph normally has one dominant job, even if it performs supporting sub-jobs. Its sentences should develop, test, illustrate, qualify or land that job.

Common paragraph jobs:

- introduce a fact, event or scene;
- explain a mechanism;
- make an inference from evidence;
- compare cases or positions;
- introduce a counterexample;
- expose a consequence or human stake;
- qualify a claim;
- shift perspective or scale;
- deliver a local payoff;
- create a justified bridge to the next move.

One paragraph may be one sentence if the sentence represents a genuine beat or turn. A long paragraph may be justified if one continuous inference requires it. Neither length is inherently good.

### 4.2 Coherence versus cohesion

- **Cohesion:** sentence-to-sentence links are visible; terms, topics and references connect.
- **Coherence:** the whole paragraph stays on one meaningful point and contributes to the larger argument.

A paragraph can be cohesive but incoherent: every sentence repeats a word from the previous one while drifting through unrelated topics. It can also be coherent but hard to follow if every sentence introduces a new topic with no bridge.

Tests:

- Write a five-to-ten-word margin label for the paragraph's actual job.
- Read only its first and last sentences. Do they concern the same main point or a deliberate progression?
- Read the opening words of its sentences. Do they form a comprehensible topic chain?
- Does every sentence support, demonstrate, complicate or land the paragraph's main move?
- If a sentence is removed, what exact inference, evidence or emotional turn disappears?

### 4.3 Paragraph movement patterns

These are reusable reasoning shapes, not mandatory templates.

**Claim → grounds → warrant → implication**  
State a bounded claim, show the evidence, explain why the evidence supports it, then show what follows.

**Observation → mechanism → consequence**  
Give a concrete event or behavior, expose the process connecting it to the outcome, and explain why the outcome matters.

**Common reading → contradiction → revised reading**  
Name the intuitive interpretation, show what it misses, and replace it with a better explanation.

**Case → distinction → transfer boundary**  
Use a case to reveal a mechanism, identify what makes it work, and state what cannot safely be generalized.

**Claim → strongest objection → adjudication**  
Give the alternative its strongest form, test it against evidence, then preserve, narrow or abandon the original claim.

**Image / moment → interpretation → return to material**  
Use a concrete image to focus attention, draw a bounded interpretation, and reconnect to the evidence or argument rather than drifting into decorative reflection.

### 4.4 Paragraph openings and endings

A paragraph opening can state its point directly, continue the prior thought, introduce a scene, pose a dilemma or create a contrast. It does not always need a textbook topic sentence.

A paragraph ending can:

- complete the inference;
- reveal the consequence;
- place a memorable detail in the stress position;
- hand a key term or unresolved consequence to the next paragraph;
- create a pause after a real turn.

Avoid endings that merely repeat the opening in more dramatic language. A transition should carry a relationship, not just a keyword.

### 4.5 Paragraph breaks and mobile rhythm

Break when the reader's task changes: new claim, new speaker, new beat, new evidence type, new perspective, meaningful contrast, or a pause that improves processing. Keep connected reasoning together when splitting would make the chain harder to reconstruct.

For mobile publishing, inspect the rendered width and scroll experience. A one-line paragraph is useful when it creates a genuine beat; if every line is isolated, emphasis becomes ordinary and the text can feel mechanically chopped.

## 5. Transitions: make the relationship visible

A transition should answer: **What is the logical relationship between the previous idea and the next one?**

| Relationship | What the next move does | Diagnostic wording (not a required phrase) |
|---|---|---|
| Continuation | Develops the same point | “That same incentive…” |
| Contrast | Shows a meaningful difference | “The chart rewards speed; creative work requires time.” |
| Concession | Accepts a limited truth before narrowing it | “That is true for early buyers, but not for every holder.” |
| Cause / consequence | Shows a supported causal relation | “Because the policy changed…” |
| Example | Makes an abstract claim observable | “One case makes the distinction visible…” |
| Elaboration | Explains a term or mechanism | “The mechanism has two parts…” |
| Scale shift | Moves from individual to system or vice versa | “At the wallet level… Across the market…” |
| Time shift | Moves to an earlier/later stage | “By the following morning…” |
| Reversal / reframe | Changes the meaning of established evidence | “The footage did not change. Its context did.” |
| Decision / implication | Converts analysis into a choice or criterion | “That leaves the organizers with a different test…” |

A useful bridge often carries one piece of old information into the next sentence or paragraph, then adds the new point. This can be a repeated key noun, a pronoun with an unambiguous referent, or a concise phrase that names the relationship.

Do not use “therefore,” “however,” “moreover,” “in fact,” or “the real issue” as generic glue. “Therefore” asserts an inference; “however” asserts a contrast; “because” asserts a cause. If the relation is not true, the transition is false even if it sounds fluent.

When a transition is difficult to write, annotate the two paragraph jobs in plain language. If you cannot explain how the second follows from the first, the problem may be missing reasoning or a bad section order—not a lack of connective words.

## 6. Details, scenes and examples

### 6.1 A telling detail must do work

A detail earns its space when it contributes to one or more of:

- orientation: where, when, what system or situation;
- evidence: a verifiable fact or observable action;
- agency: what an actor chose, did, refused or controlled;
- constraint: what limited the available options;
- mechanism: how the system actually worked;
- contrast: what differs from expectation or another case;
- emotional stakes: what was lost, risked or changed;
- memory: a concrete image that helps the reader retain the idea.

Specificity alone is not value. A vivid detail that proves nothing, changes no understanding and distracts from the reader's task is decorative load.

### 6.2 Scene, summary and exposition

- **Scene** slows down a verified moment and lets the reader follow consequential action.
- **Summary** compresses time, repeated behavior or context.
- **Exposition** explains the system, mechanism, history or meaning needed to interpret events.

Choose the tool based on reader need. A narrative feature may use more scenes; a technical explainer may need only a short case illustration; a tutorial may need a procedural demonstration rather than a dramatic scene.

Nonfiction boundary: do not invent dialogue, sensory detail, gesture, private thoughts, chronology, composite characters or author presence. If the moment is not adequately reported, summarize with attribution or state the uncertainty.

### 6.3 Examples and cases

Every example should have a declared job:

- prove or illustrate a mechanism;
- show a boundary condition;
- challenge a thesis;
- compare two outcomes;
- reveal an actor's choice;
- show how an abstract concept appears in practice.

After the example, explain what it supports and what it does not support. A single anecdote cannot establish prevalence, causality or a universal rule. Do not stack examples as a catalogue if one carefully selected case plus a real counterexample gives the reader more understanding.

## 7. Metaphor and analogy: compress structure without distorting it

Metaphor and analogy can make unfamiliar ideas easier to grasp, but they can also smuggle in false assumptions.

Before using one, specify:

1. **Target:** what real concept or mechanism needs explanation?
2. **Source:** what familiar domain is being borrowed?
3. **Mapping:** which relationships correspond—not just which objects look similar?
4. **Gain:** what does the comparison make easier to understand?
5. **Break point:** where does the analogy stop being accurate?
6. **Return:** can the article return to literal terms after the image has done its job?

A strong analogy preserves the important causal or relational structure. A weak analogy relies on superficial resemblance or imports properties the target does not have.

Use one dominant metaphor system when it helps unify the article. Avoid mixing incompatible images in a single explanatory passage. Do not let a vivid metaphor become a factual claim, and do not use a metaphor as a substitute for defining a technical mechanism.

Metaphor is not compulsory. In safety-critical instructions, legal explanation, numerical comparison or exact technical writing, literal wording may be clearer and safer.

## 8. Rhetorical devices: use form to embody thought

Rhetoric is most effective when the structure of the sentence reflects the relationship being argued.

| Device | Functional value | Use when | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| Parallelism | Makes comparable actions, criteria or outcomes easy to compare | The items genuinely share a grammatical/logical frame | Artificial symmetry hides meaningful differences |
| Anaphora | Repetition at the beginning builds accumulation or insistence | A sequence needs escalating force or a memorable cadence | Every paragraph begins the same way; chant replaces reasoning |
| Epistrophe | Repeated endings create a landing pattern | A short, bounded passage needs a deliberate refrain | Forced rhyme or slogan-like monotony |
| Antithesis | Sharpens a real opposition | The argument depends on competing incentives, states or values | False binary or repeated “not X but Y” cliché |
| Chiasmus / antimetabole | Reverses terms to expose reciprocity, feedback or role reversal | The reversal reveals a real relation | Wordplay is clever but analytically empty |
| Tricolon | Creates a memorable three-part sequence | Three items are conceptually complete or rhythmically useful | Artificially forcing everything into groups of three |
| Rhetorical question | Makes a real dilemma or inference active for the reader | The article will answer it or use it to expose a decision | Obvious question used as engagement bait |
| Motif / purposeful repetition | Lets a phrase or image gain new meaning across the article | The repeated element changes context or interpretation | Repeating the thesis without adding a new layer |
| Irony / understatement | Exposes a gap between stated meaning and actual consequence | The reader can see the discrepancy and evidence supports it | Sarcasm substitutes for explanation or punches down |
| Metaphor | Compresses a conceptual relation into an image | The mapping is clear and bounded | Decorative imagery, mixed metaphors, false equivalence |

A rhetorical figure can be removed as an ablation test. If the argument becomes clearer and loses little force, the device may be ornamental. If the comparison or causal relation becomes harder to see, the figure may be doing real cognitive work.

Do not optimize for quotable lines at the expense of the paragraph's reasoning. A “killer line” is not a substitute for the evidence that earns it.

## 9. Emotion, tension and authorial voice

### 9.1 Emotion is built from stakes, not announced with adjectives

A useful nonfiction chain is:

`verified pressure / event → observable action or attributed account → consequence → changed choice / relationship → aftershock`

Emotion can arise from fear, injustice, absurdity, relief, curiosity, admiration, grief, ambition or intellectual surprise. The article should not force every topic into outrage or a tragedy-to-hope arc.

Prefer the real consequence, choice or contradiction over a narrator announcing that something is shocking, heartbreaking or inspiring. When an emotion is not directly observable or attributed, do not write it as fact.

### 9.2 Tension is not the same as suspense

A technical article can sustain intellectual tension: a model fails, a comparison does not resolve, a risk changes the decision, or a familiar explanation cannot account for new evidence. A practical guide can sustain task tension: the reader must avoid a consequential error and needs a reliable next step.

The invariant is a meaningful unresolved problem, not an endless cliffhanger.

### 9.3 Aftershock

When an article introduces serious harm, betrayal, loss, failure or moral compromise, it should consider whether the reader needs to see the consequence persist. Do not let the article instantly reset to the next clever fact if doing so trivializes the event. Conversely, do not add a solemn aftershock when the material does not support one.

### 9.4 Voice is selection and judgment

A distinctive authorial voice emerges from:

- what the writer notices and chooses to omit;
- which questions the writer considers important;
- how the writer distinguishes evidence from interpretation;
- what the writer is willing to conclude and what remains uncertain;
- the balance of directness, humor, skepticism, empathy and restraint;
- the rhythm that follows the writer's actual thinking.

Do not manufacture voice with slang, fake vulnerability, invented biography, catchphrases, forced fragments or a global “humanizer” pass. Use owner-approved writing samples and corrections as evidence; preserve the author's actual stance.

## 10. Quotations, jargon and technical explanation

### 10.1 Quotes

A quote should do more than transfer basic information. It may reveal a speaker's distinctive language, expose disagreement, establish a consequential first-person account or provide evidence that paraphrase cannot preserve.

For each quote ask:

- Is the wording exact, contextualized and properly attributed?
- Does the quote add a voice, fact, conflict or insight that the author's prose cannot convey more clearly?
- Is the quotation too long because the writer has not yet translated the underlying idea?
- Is it placed here because this article needs it, or because the writer follows a habit?
- Would a paraphrase be more accurate and accessible?

Never invent, silently repair, splice or decontextualize quotations. Do not make sources carry the writer's explanation when the writer should explain the mechanism.

### 10.2 Jargon

Keep a technical term when it is necessary, precise and either familiar to the target reader or explained at the point of need. Define it once in plain language, then use it consistently. Do not alternate among several labels for the same concept unless the distinction matters.

A useful cold-reader test is to highlight specialist terms and ask whether a reader outside the immediate subfield can infer them from context. If not, define, translate or remove them. Do not dumb down the concept; remove avoidable decoding work.

### 10.3 Minimum viable explanation

Explain the minimum amount required for the reader to follow the article's central argument or task. Add more only when it changes the interpretation, decision or result. Introduce concepts near the point where they become useful. Break a multi-step mechanism into causal stages. Return to a key term when the reader needs to use it again.

Analogy may help, but a complete explanatory chain still needs literal terms and accurate boundaries. A reader should be able to restate the mechanism without relying on the metaphor.

## 11. Endings: close the contract, leave a residue

An ending is not the place to add a new thesis, a generic positive paragraph or an automatic call to action. It should deliver what the opening promised and leave the reader with an intentional final understanding.

Useful ending modes include:

- **Answer:** resolve the central question directly.
- **Implication:** show the consequence that follows from the argument.
- **Decision rule:** give the reader a criterion or test they can apply.
- **Return with changed meaning:** revisit an opening image, phrase or event after the reader's understanding has changed.
- **Human aftershock:** let the consequence remain visible.
- **Honest unresolved state:** name what cannot yet be settled and why.
- **Action:** invite a specific action only when it naturally follows and is genuinely useful.

Ending review:

1. Re-read the title and opening promise.
2. State what the reader should now understand.
3. Check that the body earned that understanding.
4. Remove any new claim that has not been developed.
5. Test whether the final paragraph adds a new consequence, criterion, image or implication rather than summarizing the whole article.
6. Read the final two sentences aloud. Does the last one land on the intended idea, or on incidental wording?
7. Compare at least one alternative ending when the ending materially determines the article's effect.

A circular ending is not automatically good. Returning to the opening works only if the return changes its meaning. Leaving a question open is not automatically profound; it must be a genuine unresolved issue, not an unpaid promise.

## 12. Micro-editing sequence

Do not run every diagnostic on every article. Select two to four high-risk dimensions based on the actual draft and reader job, then complete the relevant passes.

### Pass A — First-screen / opening contract

- What does the title promise?
- Does the opening orient the cold reader?
- Is the central question or contribution visible early enough?
- Is there a concrete anchor and early proof of value?
- Is the open question worth the reader's effort?

### Pass B — Paragraph function map

Write one short label for each paragraph describing what it actually does. Mark duplicates, gaps, overlong units, orphaned facts, and paragraphs that only announce importance. Reorder or rewrite before line editing.

### Pass C — Sentence flow and emphasis

For difficult passages, mark the topic/subject, main verb, old information, new information and stress position. Fix unclear actors, hidden actions, overloaded clauses and weak sentence landings. Preserve deliberate departures from the default.

### Pass D — Cohesion / transition map

Write the relationship between each pair of adjacent paragraphs: continuation, contrast, cause, example, concession, scale shift, chronology, implication or new question. If the relation cannot be named, repair the reasoning or order rather than adding a generic transition word.

### Pass E — Detail / analogy / quote audit

For every scene detail, example, metaphor and quotation, state the function it performs. Verify nonfiction provenance. Remove details that do not change understanding, evidence, choice, emotion or memory.

### Pass F — Voice / rhythm / rhetoric

Read aloud. Mark repeated sentence shapes, artificial fragments, excessive intensifiers, forced contrasts, unearned punchlines and repeated rhetorical devices. Ask whether rhythm follows changing thought or merely repeats a model cadence.

### Pass G — Ending / payoff

Compare the final page with the title, opening and reader contract. Ensure the final lines complete or intentionally bound the argument.

### Pass H — Cold-reader and rendered readback

Where possible, ask a reader unfamiliar with the subject to explain the central point, the evidence, the mechanism and the remaining uncertainty. Separately inspect the actual mobile/web rendering. A same-session model critique is self-review, not independent reader validation.

## 13. Failure modes and root-first repairs

| Symptom | Likely root | Diagnostic | Repair |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dramatic first line, weak article | Opening promise not matched by thesis/body | Hide title and first line; summarize what remains | Rebuild contract or body, not just the hook |
| Reader gets lost in sentence | New terms arrive before context; main verb delayed; clause overload | Mark old/new, subject/verb and clause hierarchy | Reorder, split or clarify actor |
| Paragraph feels smooth but says little | Cohesion without coherent paragraph job | Give it a one-line label; remove each sentence in turn | Rebuild around one meaningful move |
| Paragraph jumps abruptly | Missing logical bridge or section order defect | State relation between adjacent paragraphs | Add the actual reasoning or reorder |
| Lots of “however / therefore / moreover” | Writer is naming relations without establishing them | Replace connectors with plain-language relation labels | Repair logic; use connector only if accurate |
| Prose sounds abstract / report-like | Actors and actions hidden behind nominalizations | Highlight abstract nouns and vague subjects | Restore observable actors/actions where possible |
| Every line is short and dramatic | Cosmetic rhythm, not thought-driven rhythm | Label paragraph jobs; inspect repeated syntax | Rebuild by information function |
| Many quotes, little author voice | Sources are doing the writer's explanatory work | Identify the unique job of each quote | Paraphrase basics; keep voice-bearing evidence |
| Technical section feels like a lecture | Too much background, too early or detached from the reader question | Remove a detail and see whether the main point still works | Keep minimum viable explanation; stage the rest |
| Metaphor is memorable but inaccurate | Source/target mapping is loose | List exact correspondences and break points | Narrow or remove the analogy |
| Emotional prose feels manipulative | Adjectives or imagined interiority replace evidence | Trace each emotion to observable action or attributed account | Ground, attribute or remove |
| Ending feels pasted on | Ending not designed with reader contract | Compare opening promise with final payoff | Rebuild the ending from the article's actual contribution |
| “AI-like” prose audit becomes a blacklist | Surface symptoms mistaken for root cause | Identify the exact generic/repetitive span | Minimal semantic rewrite from material and author stance |

- The opening compresses time, money and behavior into a concrete anchor, then expands into the verified event. The crossed-out “End of story” creates a false-closure beat before the reversal.
- The article's short standalone lines are attached to actual changes in reader model or emotional beat; they are not evidence that short paragraphs should be used everywhere.
- The sentence pair “The trade advertised the story. The story advertised the trade.” makes a feedback relation visible through parallel reversal. Its value is analytical, not merely sonic.
- The image / audience reframe, community / entry-price distinction, first-joke / second-joke contrast, and final advertisement callback all reuse motifs while changing their meaning.
- The hypothetical two-screen creative choice is explicitly framed as a thought experiment. It is not presented as a reported event.
- The seven-day test converts a critical thesis into an operational proposal and observable criteria.
- The Dogecoin example acts as a bounded counterexample to total cynicism, not as proof that every token community can reproduce the result.

This is retrospective analysis of one owner-accepted work. It does not prove that the techniques caused reader retention or that the playbook improves future articles. The exact article must not become a sentence, structure or motif template.

### Rhetoric / cognition / metaphor

- Holyoak & Stamenković (2018), [Metaphor Comprehension: A Critical Review of Theories and Evidence](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000145), *Psychological Bulletin*: reviews competing accounts of metaphor comprehension; does not support treating every metaphor as a simple one-to-one analogy.
- Cheng et al. (2026), [Seductive Details, Cognitive Load, and Learning Outcomes](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-025-10099-z), *Educational Psychology Review*: meta-analysis of 50 studies / 177 effect sizes reports a small average negative effect of irrelevant seductive details on learning outcomes (g = -0.16), with moderators. This is educational-material evidence, not a direct estimate of article retention.
- Yi, Liu, Walter, Busselle & Bilandzic (2026), [Engagement Is Not One Thing](https://doi.org/10.1080/15213269.2026.2705870): meta-analysis of 52 studies / 12,705 participants separates attention, understanding, emotional engagement and presence. Associations do not prove that a particular prose device causes engagement in a specific article.
- Schneider, Beege, Nebel & Rey (2018), [A meta-analysis of how signaling affects learning with media](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2017.11.001): signals that reveal relevant information structure can help learning; transfer to prose formatting must be tested rather than assumed.

## 16. Learning and promotion gates

Current status: `CANDIDATE_REQUIRES_REVALIDATION`.

Before promotion:

1. Apply the playbook to at least two materially different, naturally occurring long-form tasks, ideally an explainer/tutorial and an argument or reported narrative.
2. Preserve exact before/after drafts and identify which microcraft mechanism was selected, why, and what was deliberately not used.
3. Include a negative case where a candidate technique is removed or shown to be inappropriate.
4. Obtain a cold read or qualified reviewer where practical; record what the reader actually understood, misunderstood, skipped or questioned.
5. Verify the method did not increase owner burden, generic voice, unnecessary length or template repetition.
6. Run repository tests and inspect the actual Skill route / knowledge-catalog entry.
7. Keep rollback simple: remove the route or revert the candidate document without affecting the CAOS control plane or existing article artifacts.

A CI pass verifies code and routing contracts, not literary quality. One owner-accepted article supports case learning, not causal proof of technique effectiveness. No metric, style detector, self-review or checklist can substitute for actual reader outcome evidence.
